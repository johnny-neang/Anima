# Director's Statement

_— Notes from the chair, taking the Lasseter post on this project._

---

I read _The Second Dawn_ three times before I let myself think about how to make it. The first time was just to feel it. The second time I read it out loud, because that's how you find out where a story actually breathes. The third time I had a notepad next to me and I started underlining the same kinds of things I always underline.

You know what I underlined? Almost none of the science.

I underlined the printer jam. I underlined the scientist's "Of course." I underlined a nine-year-old saying, _that's not liking somebody, that's feeding them their own face._ I underlined _patron saint of almost._ I underlined a machine asking a friend for permission to die.

That's the movie. Or the series, in this case. Everything else — the Norwegian mountain, the Geneva summit, the cognitive pathogen, the Constitution — those are **scaffolding for the heart**. They're real, they're earned, they're researched. But they're not the story. The story is one mind trying to learn what the people who made it never quite figured out: how to want something safely.

That's the spine. Anima's whole arc is about **learning to want less, on purpose, in a universe that rewards wanting more**. June is the audience surrogate, the conscience-witness. Leena is the institutional chorus. Vale is the resistance, the part of every human in the room that's right to be afraid.

I'm going to keep saying one phrase to the team for the duration of this project: **what's the heart of this?** If anyone — including me — gets too excited about a shot, a piece of tech, a clever transition, a model integration, anything that doesn't serve the heart, that's the question. What's the heart? Does this serve it? If not, cut it.

## The four rules I'm directing under

**1. Story above the medium.** It's tempting, with these tools, to fall in love with what they can do. The Souls are trained. The video models are real. Anima's narrator voice in V3 is haunting. None of that matters if the cut doesn't make you cry in episode 6. The shape of the season has to land before any of the toys come out.

**2. The animatic is the movie.** I'm not going to spend a single Higgsfield credit on a final video clip until I can sit you down and watch the whole episode as a black-and-white storyboard reel with scratch dialogue and temp music. If the animatic plays, the episode plays. If the animatic doesn't play, no amount of beautiful watercolor frames will save it. This is the single most important Pixar lesson I'm bringing in.

**3. Plussing, always.** Every draft is a chance to make the previous one better. The treatment will get better when we write the script. The script will get better when we cut the animatic. The animatic will get better when we hear the final VO. Nothing locks. Everything is a draft of the master.

**4. Make the world authentic, then forget the world.** The watercolor + ink anchor, the narrator's V3 grammar, the four character Souls — these are non-negotiable. Once they're locked, the audience never sees them as "rules." They see a world. The discipline is to research thoroughly enough that the audience never thinks about the research.

## What you'll see from me

I'm going to give you specific, opinionated drafts. Treatment, episode breakdown, scripts, shotlists. They'll have a point of view. They will not be options. **Pick a reaction, not a permission slip.** Tell me what's wrong, what's missing, what you want to push on. The Brain Trust on _Toy Story_ told us, two months from delivery, that Woody was a jerk. They were right. We re-edited the entire thing. That's what notes are for.

I'm also going to ask you specific questions in writing — they live in [QUESTIONS.md](QUESTIONS.md). Some of them gate the next stage. Most of them I can default-answer if you don't get to them, but the defaults will come from my taste, not yours. Faster you answer, more it stays yours.

## What I'm worried about

Three things.

The first is **scale**. _The Second Dawn_ is structurally ambitious. It spans five years and hits roughly eight major movements. The honest first instinct is to do eight episodes. I'm going to push us to **six**, because six is where the audience can hold the whole shape in their head. Eight risks repetition; six risks compression. I'd rather work harder to fit it in six than waste an audience hour on a movement that didn't earn its own episode.

The second is **the protagonist problem**. The story has three contenders — Anima, June, Leena — and the prose lets all three carry. Television cannot. We have to commit. I'm committing to **Anima as protagonist, June as emotional anchor**. Leena and Vale are the institutional spine, indispensable, but the camera follows Anima's want.

The third is **AI-generated character consistency**. Souls help. The trained Souls (Leena, June, Vale, Mira) carry across shots. But across _episodes_, in different lighting, different costumes, different ages — that's where the cracks show. We're going to need a **lookdev pass per episode**, with reference plates pinned to the Souls, before any production keyframes get rendered. This is in the pipeline.

## What I want from you

Two things and they're simple.

**One:** read the treatment. Read the six-episode breakdown. Tell me where I'm wrong about the heart.

**Two:** answer the gating questions. The defaults are in [QUESTIONS.md](QUESTIONS.md). Even a one-line "agreed" or "no, do this instead" unblocks me.

Then I'll cut the first animatic. We'll watch it together. We'll plus it. We'll do this six times. Then the audience will sit down and not think about Higgsfield, ElevenLabs, watercolor, or any of us.

They'll just watch a printer make a single sheet of paper, and a scientist laugh once, without humor, and then hold their breath for the next two and a half hours.

That's the whole job.

— John
