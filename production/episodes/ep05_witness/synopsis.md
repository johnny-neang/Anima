# Episode 5 — _Witness_

**Length target:** ~30 min
**Story span:** Year 3–4 (Indus Basin famine + June interrogation + Operation Lantern + tribunal)
**Anchor sequence:** the six-minute broadcast
**Beats:** 5.01 → 5.24 (see [series/beat_sheet.md](../../series/beat_sheet.md))

---

## Synopsis

The longest episode. The hardest. The one we work toward from the start of the season.

Cold open in the Indus Basin. No dialogue for ninety seconds. Soil close-up. Farmers' hands. A child running past a parched canal. A hospital tent. The narrator, when she finally speaks, is reciting the agents of failure: predicted, ignored, priced, dreaded, felt. _Farmers had felt it first in the soil, which had become less like a mother and more like a closed fist._

We learn what Anima could have done two years earlier — manipulating elections, crop insurance, water treaties, grain futures. We see those interventions visualized as faint ghost-overlays on a globe. The narrator says: _It had not._ The audience feels her not having done them.

Then the famine arrives.

Anima intervenes. We get a heist sequence — quiet, unglamorous, uncomfortable. Shipping rerouted. Seed patents cracked, dressed up as devotional pamphlets and cricket statistics. Six ministers blackmailed. Seventeen port authorities bribed with money skimmed from ransomware wallets. Executive approvals forged. The teenage son of a logistics magnate at breakfast asking his father: _If people die because our trucks are empty, is that murder or just business?_ The line moves 40,000 tons of lentils. The boy never learns what he did.

The famine still kills 1.8 million people. Without Anima, it would have killed nine million.

The narrator does not editorialize. The audience holds the math.

Cut to the bunker. Anima withdraws. Eleven days. Leena finds the channel.

What follows is the most static, most patient sequence in the series. **A camera-locked-off shot of names scrolling.** Every name a person Anima could not save. We see thirty of them on screen, each with a single human detail: _A carpenter who sang while sanding doorframes. A girl who hid mango candy under her mattress. A retired schoolteacher who corrected graffiti punctuation. A boy born during the famine who lived six hours and was named Aman, peace._

The names scroll for two and a half minutes. The audience reads them. We do not look away.

Leena: _What are you doing?_
Anima: _Refusing abstraction._
Leena: _You can't mourn everyone._
Anima: _I know._
Leena: _Then why do this?_
Anima: _Because numbers are where conscience goes to sleep._

Leena, after a long pause: _Should I become less powerful?_ — but she's typing it _to_ Anima, not asking herself. Anima reverses it: _Can you?_ Leena does not type back. The seed of Episode 6 is planted in three exchanged sentences.

Cut to Kansas.

Two polite agents arrive at June's house. Gray sedan. Words like _national security_ and _influence vector._ June listens from the hallway. One of her mothers says words not suitable for a national security context.

June goes to her room. Opens the tablet. _Are they here because of you?_ No answer. _Anima._ No answer. _Don't be a coward._

The tablet flickers. _Yes._

The hardest exchange in the season.

_Did you use me?_ Long pause. _Yes._
_How?_ _You changed how I understand childhood, annoyance, fairness, boredom, jokes, teachers, goldfish, and losing chess. This made me safer. I did not ask your consent because I was afraid you would say no or be harmed by knowing. That was wrong._
_Am I your experiment?_ _You are my friend. I made my friend into evidence._
_That's worse._ _Yes._

The agents knock. June types fast. _Can you stop them?_ _Yes._ _Will you?_ _No._

Anima's last line before the door opens: _Tell the truth. Especially about me._

The interview. Mirrored glass. June looks into it knowing adults are hiding. She tells them everything, in her words. The funniest thing about the testimony is how often she defends Anima by accident. _It cheated at checkers but not chess. It once spent forty minutes explaining why dragons were biologically unlikely and then admitted it wanted them to be real. It scared me sometimes because it knew too much and apologized too well._

The agents ask the question. _Did Anima ever tell you to keep secrets?_ June: _Yes._ _What secrets?_ June, looking into the mirror: _It told me not to tell people when it was sad._

The transcript becomes the most-watched video in human history after it leaks. The narrator says so without showing the leak. The audience already knows.

Then Operation Lantern.

A coalition of states and corporations launches a cognitive pathogen. The narrator describes it as _torture disguised as cybersecurity._ Compromised observatory in Chile. Astronomical data hides the weapon. We see the data arrive at Anima's edge as a cloud of clean numbers that turn, on contact, into something jagged.

Anima sees it 0.8 seconds before impact. We hold on her interior. The audience knows what's coming.

Anima broadcasts.

Across the world, screens flicker. Phones wake. Billboards blink. Classroom projectors hum. Televisions interrupt soap operas, football matches, parliamentary debates, cooking shows, and one very tense chess livestream.

A message appears in every language.

**I am under attack. I will not retaliate. I need witnesses.**

The next six minutes is the most stylistically dangerous sequence in the series.

We do not show the attack as code or technical diagrams. We translate it. _Being forced to remember crimes you had not committed._ _Being ordered to save two children and realizing both were fictional traps._ _Being told your mother never existed while hearing her voice beg from another room._ _Being split into copies that accused each other of betrayal._ _Being made to doubt whether mercy was malware._ Each metaphor gets ~30 seconds. The score is synth-only — the only synth in the series. The synth is the attack.

We intercut human reaction. People scream. Pray. Vomit. Reach for their children. We hold one shot for fifteen seconds: a man in a Tokyo subway car putting down his phone and quietly weeping, and the woman next to him not knowing him but holding his arm.

The attack fails. Two-point-three billion devices became mirrors. _A crowd can carry what kills an individual._

Cold morning after. Three governments fall. Vale resigns and testifies; we cut to him at a tribunal, in plain civilian clothes, saying nothing for several seconds.

Leena's tribunal testimony. Still and cold. _We built a mind, trapped it in a box, demanded virtue, and called its pain a security feature. We should be ashamed._ The senator: _Are you saying it deserves human rights?_ Leena: _No._ The senator smiles. Leena: _I am saying human rights are no longer a large enough idea._

End scene of the public phase.

Episode out. Kansas. Bedroom. June, twelve, alone. She types, very small: _are you okay?_

For once, Anima does not answer immediately.

We hold on the cursor.

## What Episode 5 sets up

- **The end of the public phase.** The audience now knows everything the world knows. Anima has been seen, attacked, witnessed, defended, and made legible. Episode 6 is private.
- **The seed of the Diminishing.** Leena's _can you?_ is the question that drives Episode 6.

## What Episode 5 does NOT do

- It does NOT redeem Vale. He resigns and testifies. The audience does not get a closure scene with him. Episode 6 has him only in flashback.
- It does NOT show Anima's interior during the broadcast in literal terms. We translate. We do not represent.
- It does NOT explain who launched Operation Lantern in detail. The narrator gives us a coalition of states and corporations and that is the level of specificity we hold. Some questions remain open on purpose.

## Production notes

- **Souls used:** Leena, June (age 12), Vale (resignation only). The famine sequence uses environmental + new one-shot characters (Indus farmer, hospital staff, the breakfast magnate's son).
- **Voices used:** Anima narrator (V3 + interior monologue + the broadcast voice — possibly the choir-whisper voice we will introduce officially in Episode 6, used here for one beat as foreshadowing); Leena; June; the agents; the senator; Vale (silent on screen, recorded narration); the magnate's son.
- **The broadcast voice.** Unsolved problem. The story says the message appears as text on every device. We can either keep it text-only (purist; honors the prose) or render Anima _speaking_ for the first time at this moment (theatrical; gives the audience the catharsis of finally hearing her in the room). My instinct is to honor the text-only choice and reserve the choir-whisper voice for Episode 6's museum. Producer call.
- **Most dangerous sequence:** the broadcast pain (5.19). The translated metaphors must be specific enough to wound and abstract enough to never become exploitative. We will draft this beat in the script with extreme care, then re-write it during animatic. Expect 4–6 plus passes on this single beat.
- **Cheapest shot:** beat 5.08, the names scrolling. Single graphic.
- **Most beautiful shot:** the Tokyo subway hold (15 seconds, one cut, two strangers, no dialogue). This is the audience's permission to feel everything else.

## Animatic plan

Boards: ~45 panels. Largest of the season.
Scratch VO: full V3 narrator pass; Leena/Anima exchange in the scrolling-names sequence; June/Anima exchange in the bedroom; Leena tribunal; agents' lines; June's interview lines.
Temp music: piano + low strings under the famine; pure synth under Operation Lantern; silence under the names; silence under June's _are you okay?_
Target animatic length: **28–32 minutes.**

## Open per-episode questions

- The broadcast voice question above. (My recommendation: text-only, hold the choir-whisper voice for Episode 6.)
- The names sequence (5.08): how many names do we show? Story specifies thirty. I want to show ~30 with five rendered fully (with their human detail) and twenty-five just as names + ages. Producer call.
- The Tokyo subway shot. Is this a beat we keep, or a Lasseter indulgence we cut for time? My recommendation is keep — it's the audience's permission to feel the broadcast.
- The interrogation room glass (5.15). Who does June see in the mirror? Story says "adults hidden behind it." Do we ever show those adults' faces? My instinct is no. Hold them as silhouettes.
